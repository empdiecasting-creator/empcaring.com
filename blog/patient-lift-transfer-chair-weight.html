Patient lift transfer chair weight: the frame, the load, and the push

The rating tells you who can sit in it. The frame weight tells you whether one caregiver can get it down a carpeted hallway.

What's in this guide

  • Gross, net and rated load answer three different questions
  • Net weight runs 27 to 30 kg across the three models — a three-kilogram spread
  • Why rolling resistance, not mass, is what a caregiver fights
  • A luggage-scale test you can run in two minutes
  • Loading a vehicle: the two-person rule and where to hold it
  • What a 150 kg rating covers, and the two cases it does not

Three numbers get confused on every transfer chair listing: what the chair weighs, what it ships at, and what it is rated to carry. They answer different questions. The rating tells a buyer whether the resident is within spec. The frame weight tells a caregiver whether the chair can be pushed up a carpeted hallway and lifted into a car.

This page separates them, using the figures we publish, and then deals with the part of the story that never appears on a spec sheet: effort.

Gross, net and rated load are three different numbers

Figure What it covers Who needs it
Net weightThe chair as used, with battery on the electric modelCaregivers, facility maintenance, anyone loading a vehicle
Gross weightChair plus carton and packingFreight quotes, receiving dock, courier limits
Rated loadThe resident weight the platform is rated and tested toProcurement, care planning, insurance and tenders

Mixing them up is how a facility ends up with a chair that is right on paper — 150 kg rated, well within policy — and unbearable in practice, because the pump housing and steel frame that deliver that rating also weigh 28 kg before anyone sits down.

The differences are smaller than most buyers expect — three kilograms between the lightest and heaviest. A hand-crank gearbox weighs about as much as a hydraulic pump; what the electric model adds is the battery pack and the actuator on top of the hydraulic circuit.

Why the frame weight is the number a caregiver feels

Pushing is not lifting, so raw weight is only half the story. What a caregiver actually fights is rolling resistance: the force needed to keep four small casters turning, which depends on the floor, the pile depth of the carpet and the total mass being carried — chair plus resident.

On tile or laminate, a 75 kg resident in a 28 kg chair is a one-hand push. On deep-pile carpet, the same chair needs a deliberate two-handed push, and the casters tend to follow the carpet rather than the direction you intended. Add a 2 cm carpet-to-tile threshold and the chair can stop dead — not because it is overloaded, but because a 5 cm caster cannot climb it from a standing start.

A two-minute test of your own floor

Load the chair to roughly the weight it will carry (bags of sand or water bottles work), then hook a luggage scale to the frame at push height and pull it along the actual route at walking speed. Read the force. Below about 5 kg of pull, one caregiver is comfortable. Above about 10 kg sustained, plan on two people or a different route — and check the thresholds in both directions, because that is where the number spikes.

Loading it into a vehicle

At 27–30 kg net and roughly a metre long, this is a two-person lift into anything with a boot lip: seat at its lowest to keep the centre of mass down, commode bucket removed, and hands on the frame rails rather than on the seat or the armrests. If a family is buying for home use and the chair will travel weekly, that is a reason to prefer the manual model — not because it is lighter by much, but because there is no battery to protect or charge while it is in the car.

The electric model's three extra kilograms

The battery is a 14.8 V, 2.2 Ah pack, charged from a 100–240 V adapter and topped up in about four hours. It is small, which is why the weight penalty is modest — and it is also why runtime is measured in transfers rather than hours. If the chair is used in a facility where a resident is moved a dozen times a day, put it on charge overnight rather than relying on the state of charge from the previous shift.

What the 150 kg rating does and does not cover

The rating is the resident load the platform is tested to with the seat raised, and it is identical across the three drive types. It covers the person and their clothing. It does not cover a caregiver leaning on the frame, a second person sitting on the seat edge, or loads carried on the arms while the seat is raised. Where a resident is close to the rating, the limiting factor is usually seat width and thigh support rather than the steel — which is covered in the dimensions guide.

FAQ

How much does a patient lift transfer chair weigh?

Net weight is 27 kg for the manual model, 28 kg for the hydraulic and 30 kg for the electric. Gross weight — the shipping figure, including carton and packing — is 30.5, 31 and 34.5 kg respectively. The net figure is the one a caregiver feels when pushing or loading.

What does a 150 kg rating actually mean?

It is the load the platform is rated and load-tested to with the seat raised, and it is the same rating on all three drive types. The rating covers the resident and what they are wearing; it does not cover a second person sitting or leaning on the frame, and it is not a lifting rating for the frame in transit.

Can one caregiver push a transfer chair on carpet?

On low-pile carpet and hard flooring, usually yes for a resident well under the rating. On deep pile, on a carpet with a thick underlay, or up a threshold, rolling resistance rises enough that a second person is the safer call. The rating tells you what the frame holds, not how hard it is to push.

Can I lift the chair into a car on my own?

Not recommended. At 27–30 kg net, the load is above the range most guidance treats as a single-person lift, and the shape is awkward — no balanced grip point. Two people, seat at its lowest, bucket removed, and lift from the frame rather than the seat edges.

How much does the electric model weigh compared with the manual one?

Three kilograms more: 30 kg net against 27 kg. That difference is the battery pack (14.8 V, 2.2 Ah) and the electric hydraulic actuator, and it is the part of the weight a caregiver notices when lifting rather than pushing.
