Buying patient lift transfer chairs wholesale: what actually moves the price

A unit price is four decisions wearing one number. Here is what each of them is, what a container really holds, and what to demand before the deposit leaves.

What's in this guide

  • The five variables behind any unit price
  • Why 20 units and 200 units are different products
  • Container arithmetic done from the carton, not the chair
  • What FOB leaves on your side of the invoice
  • The five documents that separate a supplier from a vendor
  • Red flags — including the "FDA approved" tell

A wholesale quote for a patient lift transfer chair is not one number. It is a unit price attached to a model, a quantity tier, a packing specification and an Incoterm — and most of the arguments that cost importers money happen because one of those four was left implicit.

This page is the buyer's half of that conversation: what actually moves the price, what a container really holds, and which documents and tests are worth insisting on before the deposit goes out.

What a factory is actually quoting

Five variables sit behind a single unit price, and each of them can be traded independently:

  • Model and drive type — manual, hydraulic and electric share a platform but not a bill of materials. The electric model carries a battery pack and an actuator.
  • Quantity tier — setup cost is spread over the run, so the curve is steep at the bottom and flat at the top.
  • Packing — a plain export carton and a retail-ready printed carton with a die-cut insert are different products with different minimums.
  • Incoterm — EXW, FOB and CIF move who pays freight and who carries risk at different points.
  • Payment terms — deposit-and-balance against a letter of credit changes the price, because it changes who finances the run.

The price ladder, and why 20 units is not 200

At low volume you are paying for setup as much as for steel: cutting and welding fixtures, powder-coat batch colour, carton printing plates, and the labour of a short run that still needs a full quality inspection. At high volume those costs disappear into the unit, and the remaining variables are material, labour and freight. This is why a 20-unit price and a 200-unit price can differ by a third, and why a factory that quotes the same number at both quantities has either not understood the question or is planning to renegotiate later.

The three questions that unlock a real quote

1. What is your MOQ per model, and does a mixed order change it?

2. What are the packed carton dimensions and gross weight for each model?

3. Is this FOB, and which port?

Container arithmetic, done from the carton

The number of chairs per container is a carton question, not a chair question. Take the packed carton volume in cubic metres, multiply by the quantity, and compare against the usable capacity of the container type you are booking — then check the weight ceiling, because this is a steel product and gross weight per unit is around 30 to 35 kg depending on model.

The practical consequence: on a heavy product, weight often runs out before volume does. A mix of manual and electric models can fit the same number of cartons but exceed the cargo weight limit sooner, so model mix belongs in the booking conversation, not after it.

Incoterms: what the importer still pays

On FOB, the factory's job ends when the goods are on board at the named port. Freight, insurance, duty, customs brokerage, port and terminal fees, and inland delivery sit with you. That is not a reason to avoid FOB — it is a reason to compare quotes at the same term. A CIF quote that looks 8% cheaper than an FOB quote is usually just hiding the freight inside the unit price, at a rate you did not negotiate.

Payment terms that survive a first order

Deposit and balance against documents is the normal shape for a first order, with a letter of credit appearing at volumes where both sides want a bank in the middle. What matters more than the split is what the balance is released against: a copy of the bill of lading and a pre-shipment inspection report are worth more than a photograph of a loaded pallet.

Red flags in a wholesale quote

  • A price with no MOQ, no packing spec and no Incoterm — you are being given the top of the range before anyone has understood the order.
  • "FDA approved" or "FDA certified". The FDA registers establishments and lists devices; it does not approve patient lifts. A supplier comfortable misstating that is comfortable misstating other things.
  • A rating with no test report behind it, or a rating that changes between the web page and the quotation.
  • Reluctance to name the port, the carton size or the packed weight — all three are known before production starts.
  • No spare-parts list. It is the cheapest thing to ask for and the clearest signal of whether the supplier expects to support the product after it ships.

What to do with the sample

A sample that arrives in one piece proves the carton works; it does not prove the chair does. Put a load on the seat, raise it to the top of its range, lower it under load, roll it over a 2 cm threshold, and check every fastener for torque marks. Then take it apart far enough to see the weld quality — the inside of a frame tells you more about a factory than the outside ever will. FAQ

What is a typical minimum order for a wholesale patient lift transfer chair?

It depends on the model and on what you are asking the factory to change. Straight production runs from existing tooling start low; a new carton print, a custom manual or a colour change pushes the floor up because each of those is a separate setup. Ask for the MOQ per model and per drive type separately — a mixed order usually has a different floor from a single-model order.

Is the unit price on an FOB quote the landed cost?

No. FOB covers the goods and getting them on board at the named port; ocean freight, insurance, duty, customs brokerage, port fees and inland delivery are all still yours. Compare quotes on the same Incoterm, and ask what the carton dimensions and gross weight are, because those decide the freight number as much as the unit price does.

How many chairs fit in a container?

Work it out from the carton, not from the chair: carton volume (CBM) × quantity against the usable capacity of a 20ft or 40HQ container, then check the weight limit — three chairs at roughly 30 kg gross each adds up faster than the volume does on this product. Ask for the packed carton size and gross weight per model and do the arithmetic before you commit to a quantity.

What documents should arrive with a wholesale order?

Commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading, and a certificate of origin if you are claiming preferential duty. On top of the shipping set, ask for the load-test report for the batch, the device listing evidence, the warranty terms, and a spare-parts list with part numbers — the last one is what separates a supplier you can live with from one you cannot.

What is a red flag in a wholesale quote?

A price with no MOQ attached, no packing specification, and no stated Incoterm. Also treat "FDA approved" or "FDA certified" claims as a warning sign: the FDA registers establishments and lists devices, it does not approve patient lifts, and a supplier who misstates that may be misstating other things too.
