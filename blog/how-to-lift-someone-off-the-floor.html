How to lift someone off the floor safely

A fall is a clinical question before it is a lifting question — then it is equipment work.

What's in this guide

  • Check for injury first: hip, head, and confusion change the plan entirely.
  • Single-handed floor lifts are the injury that care homes are trying to eliminate.
  • The floor hoist is the only device designed to lift from flat on the floor.
  • Two people and a gait belt work when the person can take weight on one leg.
  • A patient lift transfer chair is not a floor lift — it shortens the routine transfers instead.
  • Three falls in the same room is a room problem, not a training problem.

First: is this a lift, or a medical check?

If someone is on the floor after a fall, the lift is the second problem. The first is whether they should be moved at all. A fall that produced a hip fracture, a head strike, or new confusion is a call for help, not a technique — moving a resident with an unstable fracture can turn a bad afternoon into a permanent loss of mobility.

The usual triage, in this order: are they responsive and talking sense; is there pain in the hip, back, or head; can they move both arms and both legs; is there any bleeding or a bump where the head hit. If any answer is wrong, keep them still and warm and call for clinical help. Everything below assumes they are unhurt and simply cannot get themselves up.

Why "just pick them up" stopped being allowed

Care homes in the US and UK now run no-lift or minimal-lift policies, and the reason is arithmetic rather than sentiment. A 75 kg adult lifted by hand from floor level puts a load on the caregiver's lower back several times higher than the same person standing at waist height — and the caregiver is bent, twisted, and reaching, which is the worst possible combination.

The result is that floor recovery became equipment work. You either bring a machine to the resident, or you bring two trained people and a defined technique. What you do not do is improvise a single-handed pull while the resident grabs your neck.

The three real options, in order of safety

1. A floor lift (hoist) — the default answer

A portable floor hoist with a sling is the only device designed to take a person from flat on the floor back to a seated or lying position without anyone lifting. It is also the bulkiest option and the one that takes longest to deploy, which is why facilities keep one on each wing rather than one per room.

2. Two-person assisted lift with a belt

Acceptable when the resident can bear weight on at least one leg and can follow instructions. The sequence that works: roll them to the side, bring the knees up, use the arms to push up onto hands and knees, then to a kneeling position with hands on a stable chair, then one knee up, then stand with the belt. Two caregivers, one controlling the belt from behind, one steadying the chair. If any step stalls, stop and get the hoist.

3. Raising the floor — the one nobody mentions

If a resident falls repeatedly in the same spot, the cheapest fix is often a firm cushion, mat, or height-adjustable bed lowered to the floor before the fall happens. The lift gets easier when the distance is 20 cm instead of 60 cm.

Where a transfer chair fits — and where it doesn't

A patient lift transfer chair is not a floor lift. Its seat rises to bed height so a resident can slide across; it does not reach down to the floor and pick someone up. Anyone selling it as a fall-recovery device is describing a machine it isn't.

What it does is shorten the part of the job that causes the most injuries: the standing pivot from bed to chair. Once a resident is up — by hoist or by two-person technique — the transfer chair takes over the 20 or 30 transfers that follow in the next week, each of which was previously a manual lift. So the honest purchase order is: hoist for floor recovery, transfer chair for the routine moves.

What to write into the post-fall plan

  • Where the hoist lives, who checks the battery, and how it is charged — a dead hoist is the same as no hoist.
  • Two named people per shift who are trained on the belt technique, and a rule that a single caregiver never attempts a floor lift alone.
  • A written stop rule: if the resident cannot take weight on one leg, the belt method is over and the hoist comes out.
  • A fall-location log. Three falls in the same bathroom is a floor-height and lighting problem, not a training problem.

If you are buying equipment after a fall

Buy in this order and you will not overspend. First, one floor hoist for the building if there is none. Second, fix the transfers that happen every day — that is where a patient lift transfer chair pays for itself, because it removes the repeated manual pivot rather than the rare floor lift. Third, look at the room: door width, bed height, and whether the bathroom door can take a wheeled frame.

FAQ

Can one caregiver lift someone off the floor?

Not safely. A single-handed floor lift loads the caregiver's lower back far beyond safe limits and usually involves the resident pulling on the caregiver's neck. Use two trained people or a floor hoist.

Do I need a hoist if I already have a transfer chair?

They do different jobs. A hoist takes someone from the floor to a seated or lying position. A transfer chair moves someone who is already seated, between bed, bathroom and chair, without lifting.

When is it safe to use the two-person belt technique?

When the resident is unhurt, responsive, and can take weight on at least one leg. If any of those fails, stop and use the hoist.

How do I know a fall needs an ambulance?

Hip or back pain, a head strike, new confusion, vomiting, or an inability to move an arm or leg. Keep the person still and warm and call for help.

What should be in a post-fall plan?

Where the hoist is, who is trained, a written stop rule for the belt method, and a log of where falls happen so the room can be fixed.
