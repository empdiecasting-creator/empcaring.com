Hoyer lift vs patient lift transfer chair: which does this resident need?

One suspends the resident in fabric; the other slides a seat under them and lifts. Both are sold as "patient lifts", and choosing wrong is expensive in a way a spec sheet will never show you — which is why the deciding questions are about the person, not the product.

  • What each machine physically does, in one sentence each
  • The three questions that decide it — asked in this order
  • Side-by-side: labour, space, slings, cost per move
  • Why a mixed fleet is normal, and how to size the mix
  • The safety line: when a transfer chair must not be used

A sling hoist (Hoyer lift) lifts a person off the mattress while they hang in a fabric sling suspended from a boom. A patient lift transfer chair slides a split seat under them while they sit, raises that seat to bed height, and moves them across and down — no sling, nothing suspended. The first requires the resident to tolerate being carried; the second requires them to sit and be slid.

Everything else follows from that difference: which residents each suits, how many caregivers a move takes, how long a move takes, and what happens to the resident's dignity in the process.

Three questions, asked in this order

Work down this list for a specific person and stop at the first "no". It is a moving-and-handling assessment in shorthand, not a diagnosis — the care team owns the final answer.

Question If yes If no
1. Can they sit upright with support and hold their trunk for a minute? A transfer chair is possible. Go to question 2. Sling hoist. Stop here.
2. Can they tolerate being moved sideways while seated — no pain, no panic, no sudden loss of posture? Transfer chair is suitable. Go to question 3. Sling hoist, or a lateral-transfer board with two staff.
3. Does the room allow it — doorway width, turning space, bed height inside the chair's range? Transfer chair. Measure first; see below. A hoist needs floor space too — check both before buying either.

Side by side on the things that cost money

Purchase price is the smallest number in this comparison. Labour per transfer is the one that compounds across a shift, and it is where the two designs diverge most.

Dimension Sling hoist Patient lift transfer chair
Consumables Slings per resident, laundered and replaced; a sling is a hygiene item. Commodity: one chair serves many residents; buckets are the only per-resident part.
Time per transfer Positioning the sling under a dependent resident is the slow part, not the lift. Position, raise, slide, lower — typically faster when the resident can help slightly.
Caregivers One to operate, often two to sling. Designed as a one-caregiver transfer; policy decides whether that is allowed.
Floor space Must fit its legs under the bed and swing a boom. Must pass the doorway with a person on board — measure the frame, not the seat.
Where it ends up Transfers any surface to any surface, at the cost of a sling each time. Excels at bed, commode, and shower — the three places a resident actually needs to go.
Dignity Being lifted in the air is the part residents complain about most. Moves at seat level, mostly out of sight of the corridor.

The mixed fleet is the normal answer

A 60-bed building with no transfer chairs is doing a lot of unnecessary hoisting; a building with only transfer chairs is moving its dependent residents by hand, which is worse. The ratio that matters is not a rule of thumb — it is a census. Count the residents who pass all three questions above, and buy transfer chairs for that number plus a spare. Everyone else needs a hoist on each floor.

Two practical notes from the buying side. First, transfer chairs are bought in ones and twos by facilities but in tens by distributors, so lead time and reorder consistency matter more than the unit price of a single chair. Second, a chair that cannot pass the building's narrowest doorway is a chair that gets parked in a corridor — check the frame width against the actual bathroom door before the PO, not after delivery.

The safety line, stated plainly

Do not use a transfer chair for a resident who cannot sit unsupported, cannot be slid without pain or distress, or whose weight exceeds the tested rating of the chair. There is no workaround for any of those three. A transfer chair is not a lighter-duty hoist; it is a different class of device that assumes a different level of function.

The corollary matters just as much: hoisting a resident who can sit and slide is not "safer by default". It costs more time, more sling laundry, and more of the resident's dignity — and it moves the risk onto the caregiver's shoulders during sling placement. Match the device to the person.

What goes in the care plan either way

  • The device by name and asset number, not the category, so a shift change cannot substitute one for the other.
  • Height settings in centimetres. The bed height, the commode height, and the seat range of the chair — the number that makes the transfer work, written down.
  • Number of staff required, per the assessment, and whether a second person must be in the room.
  • The falls-and-near-misses record. If a transfer is producing incidents, the assessment was wrong or the equipment does not fit the room.

FAQ

Is a Hoyer lift the same as a patient lift?

Hoyer is a brand name that became the generic word for a mobile sling hoist, in the way that Hoover became the word for a vacuum. So a Hoyer lift is a patient lift of the sling-hoist type. It is not a transfer chair: a transfer chair moves the resident seated, without a sling, and does not suspend them in the air.

Can a transfer chair replace a Hoyer lift?

Only for residents who can sit upright with support and tolerate being moved sideways. For someone who cannot bear weight, cannot hold their trunk, or is too heavy for a seated slide, a sling hoist is the safe option and a transfer chair is not a substitute. In most facilities the two coexist rather than compete.

Which is cheaper to own, a Hoyer lift or a transfer chair?

A transfer chair is usually cheaper on both counts: lower purchase price for a comparable capacity, and less labour per transfer because it needs no sling fitting and no second pair of hands for a standard move. A Hoyer lift costs more per move in caregiver time, which is the line item that dominates in a facility.

Do you need two caregivers for each type?

A sling hoist is usually a one-caregiver operation once the resident is slinged, but sling placement under a resident who cannot roll often needs two. A transfer chair is designed as a one-caregiver transfer for a resident who can assist slightly. Local policy and the resident's care plan decide, not the equipment alone.

What should a facility buy first?

Buy for the residents you actually have, not for the category. Count how many residents can sit with support and tolerate a sideways slide — that number justifies transfer chairs. The rest need hoists, and a building with no hoist will be moving those residents manually, which is the injury risk that costs more than either device.
