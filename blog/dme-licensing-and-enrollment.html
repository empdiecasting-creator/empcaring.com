Selling DME in the US: the three layers between a product and a paid invoice

Medicare, the FDA and your state each ask for something different. Most new distributors discover that in the wrong order.

What's in this guide

  • Why "can I sell it" and "can I bill for it" are different questions
  • The four requirements of Medicare DMEPOS enrolment
  • What FDA registration does and does not mean
  • Why there is no national DME licence
  • The facility route, and what it swaps out
  • Five documents to demand before the first order

Distributors ask one question in two different ways: "can I sell this?" and "can I bill for this?". They are not the same question, they are not governed by the same agency, and conflating them is how a new DME business buys stock it cannot invoice.

This page separates the three layers — Medicare, FDA, and the state — and then lists what a manufacturer should hand over before the first order ships. It is a map of the questions, not legal advice; every figure and rule below is one you should verify with your own advisers before relying on it.

Layer one: billing Medicare

If you intend to bill Medicare for durable medical equipment, you are enrolling as a DMEPOS supplier. In practice that means working through four requirements, in roughly this order:

  • Enrolment through the National Supplier Clearinghouse, submitted electronically — the application is the gate everything else hangs on.
  • Accreditation from a CMS-approved accreditation organisation, covering the product categories you intend to bill.
  • A surety bond — $50,000 is the figure that applies to DMEPOS suppliers under the competitive bidding programme. Verify the current requirement for your enrolment type.
  • Liability insurance, a physical location and a site inspection — the parts of enrolment that surprise people who assumed it was paperwork only.

Layer two: the FDA, which is about the product

The FDA layer applies whether or not you ever bill Medicare. A patient lift transfer chair is a Class I device: the establishment that makes it registers with the FDA and lists the device, and it does so without any "approval" step. There is no FDA certificate for this product, which is why the phrase "FDA approved" in a supplier's email is a reliable signal about how carefully the rest of the paperwork was done.

Two practical consequences for a distributor: first, the importer of record has its own registration obligations, separate from the manufacturer's; second, labelling and instructions travel with the device, so a private-label manual is a regulated document, not a marketing one.

Layer three: the state you trade in

There is no national DME licence. States differ substantially: some license DME suppliers outright, some regulate specific product categories, and some require nothing beyond a business registration and a sales-tax permit. The only reliable method is to check your own state's licensing authority before you accept the first order — and to check again if you start shipping across state lines, because the rules that apply to a walk-in sale and an interstate shipment are not always the same.

If you sell to facilities instead

Many distributors never enroll with Medicare at all: they sell to nursing homes, assisted living and hospitals, and get paid by the facility. That route swaps federal enrolment for vendor credentialing — certificates of insurance, a W-9, product liability cover, sometimes a group purchasing organisation contract, and a payment-terms conversation. It is a lower barrier and a different margin structure, and it is the route most new entrants should cost out first.

The four mistakes that cost the most

  1. Buying stock before checking enrolment. Inventory you cannot invoice is working capital sitting on a shelf.
  2. Reading "FDA registered" as "approved". It is a factual statement about an establishment and a listing, and it has to be reproduced accurately in your own marketing.
  3. Assuming the manufacturer's accreditation covers you. Theirs covers their facility; your enrolment is yours, and so is your bond.
  4. Treating state law as an afterthought. The rules that decide whether you can trade are the ones nobody puts in a product brochure.

FAQ

Do I need a licence to sell DME in the United States?

It depends on the state and on what you are selling. Some states license DME suppliers outright, others regulate only certain categories or require nothing beyond a business registration and a sales-tax permit. There is no single national DME licence: the federal layer is about billing Medicare, and the state layer is about trading. Confirm both before you take a first order.

What is the difference between enrolling with Medicare and being FDA registered?

They are unrelated regimes. Medicare enrollment is about being allowed to bill a federal payer: it runs through the National Supplier Clearinghouse and requires accreditation and a surety bond. FDA establishment registration and device listing are about the product being made and placed on the market by a registered establishment. A distributor who never bills Medicare still sells an FDA-registered, listed device.

How much is the surety bond for a DMEPOS supplier?

The bond required of DMEPOS suppliers under the competitive bidding programme is $50,000. Treat that as the current figure to verify rather than a permanent one, and confirm the requirement that applies to your own enrolment type and state before budgeting.

Can I resell a transfer chair without accreditation?

You can sell to consumers and facilities without Medicare accreditation, as long as you are not billing Medicare for the device. Accreditation is tied to Medicare enrolment. Selling to facilities instead brings a different set of requirements — vendor credentialing, insurance certificates and often a group purchasing contract.

What paperwork should the manufacturer give a new distributor?

At minimum: evidence of establishment registration and device listing, a load-test report for the model, the label and manual as shipped, warranty terms, a spare-parts list with part numbers, and the packing specification. If a factory cannot produce the first two in writing, the rest of the relationship will be improvised too.
