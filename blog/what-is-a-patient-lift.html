What is a patient lift? Types, and how it differs from a transfer chair

Four different machines are sold under the same three words, and the difference is not cosmetic — it decides whether a resident can be moved at all. Here is what a patient lift actually is, what the alternatives are, and how to tell which one a given person needs.

What's in this guide

  • What "patient lift" usually means, and the three other machines that share the name
  • A side-by-side of sling hoist, sit-to-stand lift, ceiling lift, and seat-lift transfer chair
  • How to pick by the resident's ability, not by the product name
  • Why "patient lifter" and "patient hoist" mean the same thing, and "transfer chair" does not
  • The five specifications worth checking before you buy either type
  • When a transfer chair is the wrong answer and a sling hoist is the only safe one

Ask five people in a care facility what a patient lift is and you will get three different machines. To a nurse it usually means a sling hoist — the frame with a boom and a fabric sling that lifts someone out of bed. To a home care aide it can mean the sit-to-stand device that helps a person pull themselves up. To a DME catalogue it often includes seat-lift transfer chairs, which do not lift the person at all in the usual sense: they lift a seat to bed height so the resident can slide across.

The confusion is not harmless. A family buys the wrong one because the product page used the same word, and the equipment arrives unable to do the job. So this guide starts with the distinction rather than the sales pitch.

The short answer: the name covers four machines

In US care settings, "patient lift" most often means a sling hoist — sometimes called a Hoyer lift, after the brand that became generic. It lifts a person suspended in a fabric sling. Everything else that gets called a patient lift either assists a standing transfer or moves a seated person sideways. The table below is the whole taxonomy in one place.

Machine How it moves a person Who it suits
Sling hoist (Hoyer) Lifts the person suspended in a sling on a boom. Non-weight-bearing, cannot sit unsupported, or too heavy for a seated transfer.
Sit-to-stand lift Supports the trunk while the person pulls themselves upright and pivots. Can bear some weight and follow instructions; used for toileting and short transfers.
Ceiling lift Same sling principle, but the hoist runs on a track above the bed. Same residents as a sling hoist, in a room where a track can be installed.
Seat-lift transfer chair Raises a commode seat to bed height; the resident slides across while seated, then the seat lowers. Can sit with support and tolerate a sideways slide; needs bed-to-bathroom movement, not a hoist.

A sling hoist and a seat-lift transfer chair are bought by the same buyer, for different residents, from the same catalogue section — which is exactly why the vocabulary merges in practice.

How to choose by the resident, not the product

The deciding question is not "which lift is best" but "what can this person still do". Work down this list and the category usually picks itself:

  1. Can they bear weight through their legs and follow a cue to stand? If yes, a sit-to-stand lift or a supervised standing transfer may be enough — and it keeps more of their own function in use.
  2. Can they sit upright with support and hold their trunk while sliding sideways? If yes, a seat-lift transfer chair works, and it solves the bathroom trip as well as the bed exit.
  3. Can they not sit unsupported, or not tolerate a slide? Then a sling hoist is the safe answer, regardless of how convenient a transfer chair would be.
  4. Is the room the constraint? A hoist needs floor space to position its legs under the bed; a transfer chair needs a doorway wide enough for its frame. Measure both before choosing.

Patient lifter, patient hoist, transfer chair: why the words blur

Patient lifter and patient hoist are regional variants of the same thing as patient lift — lifter is the American usage, hoist is British and Australian. If a page or a quote uses either word, assume a sling device unless it says otherwise.

Transfer chair is the term that genuinely means something different. It describes equipment that moves a person while seated — from bed to bathroom, bed to chair — without suspending them. In retail listings you will also see commode transfer chair and lift commode chair: both describe the same seat-lift design, because the commode opening is part of the machine rather than an accessory. And in DME catalogues you will find patient lift transfer chair, which is the name the US market settled on for this category even though it is not a sling lift at all.

The five specifications worth checking — either type

Whichever machine you are looking at, these five numbers decide whether it works in the room it is going into. Marketing language does not survive contact with a doorway.

  1. Height range. For a hoist, the maximum lift height and the leg clearance under the bed. For a transfer chair, the seat-height range against the actual bed height — for example a 45–63 cm manual seat against a bed at 60 cm.
  2. Width against the narrowest doorway. A 74 cm frame does not pass a 76 cm opening with a person's hands on the armrests. Measure the door, subtract two inches, then compare.
  3. Weight of the machine itself. The rating tells you what it can carry; the product weight tells you whether one caregiver can push it over carpet.
  4. Safe working load, with a test record. Around 150 kg for standard models. Ask for the test method and the result, not a badge.
  5. Power source. Battery, foot pump, or hand crank. Manual and hydraulic models work in a bathroom with no socket and during a power cut — which is not a hypothetical in home care.

When a transfer chair is the wrong answer

This is the part a sales page will not tell you. A seat-lift transfer chair is faster, cheaper to own, and far less intrusive than a hoist — but it assumes three things: the resident can sit with support, they can tolerate being moved sideways, and their weight is within the rating. If any of those is false, the correct equipment is a sling hoist, and using the chair anyway is how caregivers get hurt and residents get dropped.

The honest framing for a facility is that the two categories are complementary, not competing. A building with 60 residents will own hoists for the residents who need them and transfer chairs for the ones who do not — and the second group is usually larger than expected, because "can sit with support" covers most people who cannot walk to the bathroom.

FAQ

What is a patient lift?

In most of the US care industry a patient lift means a sling hoist: a frame with a boom and a fabric sling that lifts a person out of bed and lowers them into a chair, bath, or vehicle. The same words are also used loosely for sit-to-stand lifts, ceiling lifts, and seat-lift transfer chairs, which work in completely different ways. The mechanism, not the name, determines who can safely be moved with it.

What is a patient lifter?

Patient lifter is an American English variant of patient lift and usually refers to the same sling hoist. In the UK and Australia the equivalent term is patient hoist. None of these terms implies a seat-lift transfer chair unless the seller says so explicitly.

What are the types of patient lifts?

Four machines share the name: a sling hoist (mobile frame with a boom and sling), a sit-to-stand lift (assists a person who can bear some weight to pull themselves upright), a ceiling lift (a hoist mounted on a track above the bed), and a seat-lift transfer chair (a wheeled commode chair whose seat rises to bed height so the resident can slide across). Each fits a different level of mobility.

Is a patient lift the same as a transfer chair?

No. A sling hoist lifts the person suspended in fabric; a seat-lift transfer chair raises a seat to bed height and the resident slides across while seated. The transfer chair is used for people who can sit with support and tolerate the sideways transfer. A sling hoist is required when they cannot bear weight, cannot sit unsupported, or are too heavy for a seated transfer.

How much weight can a patient lift hold?

Most mobile sling hoists and most seat-lift transfer chairs are rated around 150 kg (330 lb), with bariatric versions above that. The rating is a tested safe working load, not a suggestion — check the load-test documentation rather than the catalogue description, and confirm the rating applies to the specific configuration you are buying.

Does a patient lift need electricity?

Only electric models do. Manual and hydraulic lifts are powered by the caregiver, which matters during a power cut or in a bathroom without a socket. Electric models typically run on a small rechargeable battery with a mains charger and manage multiple transfers per charge.
