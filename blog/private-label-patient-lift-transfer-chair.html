Private label patient lift transfer chairs: what is actually brandable

Badges and cartons are marketing. Manuals and packing are liability. Here is the split, and the five files to lock before a production run starts.

What's in this guide

  • What a private label run can change without tooling
  • The three sentences in a manual that carry the legal risk
  • Five artwork files to lock before production
  • The approval gate that buyers skip and regret
  • What packing has to survive at 30–35 kg gross
  • Who owns the artwork, the die lines and the pack

Private label sounds like one decision and is really a list: what carries your name, what the carton says, what the manual claims, what the barcode encodes, and what the factory is allowed to change without asking. Get the list right and a private-label run is a marketing exercise. Get it vague and the first container arrives with a badge that does not sit flat and a manual that names the wrong importer.

This is the checklist we run with distributors, written from the factory side of the table.

What is brandable, and what is not

Item Brandable now Needs tooling
Badge or label plateYes — laser etch, printed plate or moulded badgeOnly if the badge shape changes
Carton printYes — flexo print on the existing die lineNew die line for a new carton shape
Manual, warranty card, insertYes — your copy, your brand, our templateNo
Barcode / GTIN on the cartonYes — your GS1 number, printed at the size the retailer specifiesNo
Frame colourYes, but as a powder-coat batchNo tooling, but a batch minimum
Frame geometry, welding, drive layoutNoYes — fixtures, testing, a different MOQ entirely

The manual is the part that creates liability

Everything else on this page is a marketing decision. The manual is not. Three sentences get copied into every private-label manual we produce, and they are the three that matter:

  • What the device is: a seat-lift transfer chair for bed-to-chair transfers and toileting, with a stated seat range and rated load.
  • What it is not: not a sling hoist, not a substitute for a moving-and-handling assessment, not for use by a resident outside the stated weight.
  • What the regulatory status is: the establishment is FDA registered and the device is listed. That is the accurate sentence, and it is the only one that should appear — "FDA approved" belongs nowhere near a patient lift, and a brand owner who ships it in their own manual owns that claim.

Artwork: the five files to lock before production

  1. Vector logo with a one-colour version for the badge etch.
  2. Carton artwork on the die line the factory supplies — not on a die line drawn from a photograph.
  3. Manual in the final language, with the labelled diagrams re-exported if the model changes.
  4. GS1 barcode at the size and position the retailer requires, with the human-readable digits under it.
  5. Colour references as Pantone or RAL values, plus a physical proof if the colour is brand-critical.

The timeline, stage by stage

A single lead-time figure hides the only part of a private-label order that buyers get wrong: the approval gate. Four stages, each with its own clock and its own risk:

  • Artwork and die line — fastest stage, and the one where a wrong file is cheapest to catch.
  • Printed sample — a carton and a badge, signed off in your hand. Skipping this is how a container arrives with a logo in the wrong place.
  • Production — the run itself, including the load test and the packing spec check.
  • Booking and documents — the stage where a missing certificate of origin costs a week nobody planned for.

Packing: the part buyers underestimate

A steel chair at 30 to 35 kg gross travels badly if the carton is designed for a photograph. Ask what the drop-test standard is for the carton, what the internal bracing looks like, and whether the carton has a pallet footprint that your receiving dock can handle. On an e-commerce programme a damaged-on-arrival rate is a returns cost, and returns on a 30 kg item are not a rounding error.

Who owns what, in writing

Two sentences in the supply agreement prevent most private-label disputes: the brand owner owns the artwork and receives the print-ready files; the die lines and any tooling the factory paid for remain with the factory, but the brand owner is entitled to a reproduction of the pack from files they hold. If a second factory could not reproduce your carton from what sits on your own server, you do not own your packaging.

FAQ

What can actually be branded on a private-label transfer chair?

The label plate or badge, the carton print and inserts, the manual and warranty card, and the barcode or GTIN on the carton. The frame geometry, the welding and the drive mechanism are production items, not branding ones — changing those means tooling, not artwork, with a different minimum and a different timeline.

What is the usual minimum for a private-label run?

Lower than most buyers expect for artwork-only changes, higher for anything that touches tooling or a new colour batch. Ask for the minimum per model and per drive type, and ask specifically whether a mixed-brand carton programme (same carton, different sticker or print run) has a lower floor than a full print change.

Can the manual say the product is FDA approved?

No — and a supplier who writes that into a manual is creating a liability for the brand owner. The FDA registers establishments and lists devices; it does not approve patient lifts. Manuals can state that the establishment is FDA registered and the device is listed, and should say exactly that, no more.

How long does a private-label order take from artwork to shipment?

Plan in stages rather than one number: artwork and die-line approval, a printed sample for sign-off, then production, then booking. Each stage has its own clock and the sample stage is where most of the calendar goes. Ask for a dated plan with the approval gate named on each line, not a single lead-time figure.

Who owns the artwork and the carton die lines?

Agree it in writing before the first order. The normal arrangement is that the brand owner owns the artwork and the factory holds the die lines it paid to produce; what matters is that a second factory could reproduce the pack from files you hold, not from files the factory holds.
