Bed to wheelchair transfer: technique, then equipment

Most transfers fail on geometry, not on strength. Measure first, choose the method second, and touch the resident third.

What's in this guide

  • A bed above knee height turns a sit-to-stand into a lift. Lower the bed before positioning the chair.
  • A seat 5 cm below the mattress is a controlled slide. Ten centimetres below is a drop.
  • Remove the armrest on the transfer side and lock both brakes before anything else happens.
  • Lifting under the arms loads the resident's shoulder and the carer's back at the same moment. Use a gait belt.
  • If the destination is the toilet, a standard wheelchair adds two avoidable transfers to the trip.

Most bed-to-wheelchair injuries in care settings are not caused by a weak resident. They are caused by a wheelchair that cannot get close enough to the bed, a bed left at mattress height, an armrest in the way, and a carer improvising a pivot before breakfast.

The order matters: measure first, decide the method second, and only then touch the resident. Four numbers decide which methods are even available to you.

The four measurements before you touch anything

Measurement Why it decides the method
Bed height against the resident's knee A bed above knee height turns a sit-to-stand into a lift. Lower the bed before you position the chair.
Wheelchair seat height against mattress height A seat five centimetres lower than the mattress is a controlled slide. Ten centimetres lower is a drop.
Gap between mattress edge and chair Under ten centimetres is workable. Beyond that, the resident bridges the gap with their arms instead of their legs.
Floor space behind the chair The carer has to stand beside and slightly behind the resident, not in front of them.

Standing pivot: the eight steps

This is the method to use when the resident can take weight through their legs and follow a two-step instruction. It is the fastest method, and the one most often done badly.

  1. Lock both wheelchair brakes and flip the footplates up.
  2. Remove the armrest on the transfer side, or swing it back.
  3. Lower the bed until the mattress sits just above the chair seat.
  4. Position the chair at a 20 to 30 degree angle to the bed, not parallel to it.
  5. Have the resident shuffle to the mattress edge, feet flat, stronger leg set back.
  6. Put your hands on the resident's hips or on a gait belt. Never under the arms.
  7. Count out loud: on three, the resident leans forward, pushes, and you guide the hips.
  8. Let them reach back for the far armrest and lower themselves. Do not lower them.

Slide board: when it is the right tool

A slide board is the correct answer when the resident can sit upright but cannot reliably bear weight: a hip replacement on day two, advanced Parkinson's, anyone whose blood pressure drops on standing. It is the wrong answer when the resident cannot sit unsupported, because then two people are managing a board and a falling torso at the same time.

The board also needs the same clearance as any other method. A board that spans a 15 cm gap is a bridge with a resident on it.

When the wheelchair is the wrong equipment

If the destination is the toilet, a standard wheelchair is a detour: bed to chair, chair to bathroom, chair to toilet, then the same sequence back. Every one of those is an exposure. A patient lift transfer chair collapses the sequence into one movement. The seat rises to bed height, the resident slides across, the seat lowers over the commode, and the trip happens once instead of four times.

The arithmetic that decides whether a wheelchair fits the bedside also decides whether a transfer chair fits the bathroom door. Our three models measure 61 cm, 52 cm and 53 cm across the frame, against a typical 76 cm clear opening. The equipment check that takes thirty seconds

  • Brakes. Push the chair with the brakes on. If it rolls, the transfer does not start.
  • Tyres. A flat caster on a tiled floor slides sideways during the pivot.
  • Armrest. Removable on the transfer side, or the resident climbs over it.
  • Footplates. Up and out of the way, or the resident's feet catch on them on the way up.
  • Gait belt. On the resident before they stand, not after they wobble.

Frequently asked questions

Can one carer do a bed-to-wheelchair transfer alone?

Yes, if the resident can take weight through their legs and follow instructions. If they cannot, one carer is not a staffing decision, it is a fall waiting for a date.

Is it safe to lift a resident under the arms?

No. Lifting under the arm loads the shoulder joint and the carer's lower back at the same time. Hands go on the hips or on a gait belt.

How high should the bed be for a transfer?

Lower it until the mattress is just above the wheelchair seat, so the resident slides rather than drops.

Can a transfer chair replace a wheelchair entirely?

For toileting, showering and short in-room moves, often yes. For distance down a corridor or outdoors, a wheelchair remains the right vehicle.

What if the wheelchair brakes do not hold?

Stop. Tag the chair out and get it serviced before the next transfer.
