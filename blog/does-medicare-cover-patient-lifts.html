Does Medicare cover patient lifts? What families need to know

Medicare Part B does cover patient lifts — but the conditions attached to that yes trip up more families than the paperwork itself. The five requirements, the billing codes, the lift-chair mix-up, and an honest look at when factory-direct is the better math.

What's in this guide

  • Medicare Part B covers patient lifts as durable medical equipment (DME) when medically necessary and prescribed for home use
  • You need a face-to-face evaluation and a written order from your doctor before a supplier can bill Medicare
  • The lift must come from a Medicare-enrolled DME supplier — buy outside that system and there is no reimbursement
  • With Original Medicare you pay the annual Part B deductible, then 20% of the Medicare-approved amount
  • Lift chairs are different: Medicare covers only the seat-lift mechanism, not the chair around it
  • Factory-direct purchases — ours included — are self-pay; they make sense when the all-in cost beats your coinsurance and rental limits

The quick answer: yes — Medicare Part B covers patient lifts as durable medical equipment when the lift is medically necessary. But the conditions attached to that yes are where most families trip up. The lift has to be prescribed after a face-to-face evaluation, supplied by a Medicare-enrolled DME supplier, documented as medically necessary, and used in your home. Miss one, and the claim can be denied — after the lift is already in your living room.

This guide is for the family caregiver working out whether insurance will help pay for a lift — with a manufacturer's view of the equipment side. We build patient lift transfer chairs in Zhaoqing, China, and ship factory-direct into the US market, so we sit on the supply side of this question daily. We will be blunt about what Medicare covers, what it does not, and where buying direct fits.

What makes a patient lift "covered" DME

Medicare Part B pays for equipment it classifies as durable medical equipment — a four-part test, not a judgment about how useful the device is.

Medicare's four DME criteria:

  1. Durable. The device can withstand repeated use. A lift built for daily transfers over years passes; a disposable item does not.
  2. Primarily medical. It is used mainly for a medical reason. A lift ordered because a family member cannot be transferred safely is medical; a recliner bought for comfort is not.
  3. Used in the home. The equipment has to be used in the residence — which is why the order and the supplier's paperwork always reference home use.
  4. Normally not useful to someone without an illness or injury — a transfer lift has little value to a person who can stand and pivot unaided.

A floor-style patient lift — mast, boom, and sling — clears all four tests without controversy, which is why it sits firmly inside the DME benefit. For the full definition, and how products like the E0630 hydraulic lift map onto it, see our guide to what counts as durable medical equipment.

The 5 requirements for Medicare coverage

Meeting the DME definition gets the lift into the covered category. Getting a specific claim paid depends on five requirements — and all five have to line up.

The 5 requirements:

  1. A face-to-face evaluation and a written order from your doctor. Your doctor or treating provider must evaluate you in person and issue a written order before a lift can be billed. A phone recommendation from the family does not substitute.
  2. A Medicare-enrolled DME supplier. The lift has to come from a supplier enrolled in Medicare, who bills Medicare directly and holds the documentation. Buy from a site that is not enrolled, and there is no claim to file.
  3. Documentation of medical necessity. Your doctor's records must explain why you need a lift — for example, that you cannot be transferred between bed and chair safely without one. Thin documentation is the most common reason claims fail.
  4. Use in the home. Medicare's DME benefit is written around home use. If you are weighing home care against a facility, the equipment and payment picture changes — we compare the two settings in our guide to patient lifts at home vs in a nursing home.
  5. The right plan rules. On Original Medicare, standard Part B DME rules apply. In a Medicare Advantage plan, DME must be covered at least as well — but the plan can add network and authorization requirements, covered below.

What Medicare actually pays

For covered DME, Original Medicare pays 80% of the Medicare-approved amount after you have met the annual Part B deductible. You are responsible for the remaining 20% coinsurance unless supplemental coverage picks it up. Both the deductible and the approved amount change every year — check Medicare.gov for the current figures before you order.

The other wrinkle: Medicare does not always buy equipment outright. For some categories it pays a monthly rental for a set period instead of a purchase price, depending on the item's classification and the supplier's arrangement. Ask up front whether your lift will be rented or sold, what happens at the end of a rental, and what servicing is included — those answers affect your real cost more than the headline 80/20 split.

The HCPCS codes to know

Every claim runs through a billing code. Three codes come up again and again:

HCPCS code What it is What families should know
E0630 Patient lift, hydraulic, with seat or sling The manual floor lift — typically the lower-cost option; no batteries or charging.
E0635 Patient lift, electric, with seat or sling The powered floor lift — a motor raises the boom; less effort per transfer, higher price.
E0620 Seat-lift mechanism The lift-chair component only — the motorized seat tilt. The chair around it is not covered.

Slings and similar accessories are billed separately — budget for them, and confirm the sling size and style are on the order. Stand-assist (sit-to-stand) lifts fall under their own DME codes; since coverage turns on the patient's weight-bearing ability, have your supplier confirm the exact code before ordering.

Choosing between hydraulic and electric is a separate decision from coverage — both are coverable. We compare them in our guide to manual, hydraulic, and electric patient lifts.

The lift chair confusion

Searches for Medicare coverage collide with a second product: the lift chair — the padded recliner whose seat tilts forward to help someone rise to standing. Families are told lift chairs are covered, then discover the fine print: Medicare covers only the seat-lift mechanism (HCPCS code E0620) — the motorized part that raises the seat. The chair around it is not covered.

In practice, Medicare pays a small fraction of the chair's cost. The approved amount applies to the mechanism alone; frame, upholstery, and recline functions are entirely out of pocket. It is the single most common Medicare lift misconception we hear.

The two products solve different problems. A lift chair assists one motion — rising from a seated position — for someone who can still bear weight. A patient lift transfers someone who cannot safely stand at all, moving them by sling between bed, chair, and commode. If your family member cannot assist in the transfer, the lift chair is the wrong product to discuss with Medicare; the lift is the right one.

Medicare Advantage — the fine print

In a Medicare Advantage (Part C) plan, federal rules require DME coverage at least as generous as Original Medicare. What the plan can add: a network of approved suppliers, prior authorization, and its own out-of-pocket structures. In practice, the lowest-premium plan can be the most-paperwork plan.

Before ordering, call the number on your plan card and ask: which suppliers are in network for DME, whether prior authorization is required for a patient lift, and what your cost share will be. Get the answers in writing before the lift is delivered, not after.

What Medicare usually does not cover

Three situations come up repeatedly:

Usually not covered:

  • Lifts ordered for convenience. If the record shows the lift is primarily a convenience rather than a documented medical need, the claim fails the medical necessity test.
  • Ceiling lifts. Overhead ceiling lifts are rarely covered under the standard DME benefit; specialty programs sometimes differ. Confirm before anyone quotes you a ceiling installation.
  • Equipment bought outside the enrolled-supplier system. If the lift did not come through a Medicare-enrolled supplier — including direct imports and factory-direct orders — Medicare will not reimburse it.

A ceiling lift may be right for a two-person transfer in a small bedroom; a factory-direct lift may be right for the budget. These are self-pay decisions to make with eyes open — not claims denied after the money is spent.

FAQ

Does Medicare cover patient lifts?

Yes. Medicare Part B covers patient lifts as durable medical equipment when medically necessary — prescribed after a face-to-face evaluation, documented in your records, used in your home, and supplied by a Medicare-enrolled DME supplier. Original Medicare pays 80% of the approved amount after the annual Part B deductible; you pay 20% unless supplemental coverage picks it up.

How much will I pay out of pocket?

With Original Medicare, you pay the annual Part B deductible first, then 20% of the Medicare-approved amount. Both figures change each year — check Medicare.gov or call 1-800-MEDICARE for current numbers. A Medigap or Medicare Advantage plan may lower your share, but an Advantage plan may require in-network suppliers or prior authorization, so ask your plan before ordering.

Does Medicare cover stand-assist / sit-to-stand lifts?

Stand-assist (sit-to-stand) lifts can also be covered when the same conditions are met: a face-to-face evaluation, a written order, medical necessity documentation, and a Medicare-enrolled supplier. Coverage depends on the model and the patient's ability to bear weight and assist during the stand, so ask your supplier to confirm which HCPCS code applies and document the medical necessity.

Does Medicare cover lift chairs?

Not the whole chair. Medicare covers only the seat-lift mechanism (HCPCS code E0620) — the motorized part that tilts the seat forward to help a person rise. The chair itself — frame, upholstery, recline motor — is not covered, and the approved amount for the mechanism is far less than the chair's price, so you pay the difference. It is the most common Medicare lift misconception we hear.

Can I get reimbursed if I buy directly from an overseas factory?

In most cases, no. Medicare pays Medicare-enrolled suppliers, who bill Medicare directly and handle documentation, rental-versus-purchase rules, and servicing. A factory-direct purchase — including a lift imported from a manufacturer overseas — is self-pay, and Medicare generally will not reimburse equipment bought outside that system. Factory-direct makes sense when you are paying your own way and the total cost beats your projected coinsurance, rental fees, and model limitations.

The bottom line

Medicare does help pay for patient lifts — inside a system of orders, enrolled suppliers, documentation, and home use. Work it when reimbursement matters; when speed, model choice, or total cost matters more, price the self-pay route all-in, not sticker.
