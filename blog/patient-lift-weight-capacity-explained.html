Patient lift weight capacity explained: what 150 kg really means

150 kg is the number on the spec sheet — but what does it actually cover, what doesn't it cover, and how do you match it to your resident population? This is the question procurement managers should ask before signing the PO. Here is the answer, from the factory floor.

What's in this guide

  • What "150 kg weight capacity" actually means — rated load vs. ultimate load vs. safety margin
  • The three numbers that matter: rated load, dynamic load, and safety factor (with a comparison table)
  • How 150 kg maps to real adult weight percentiles by gender and region
  • What happens when you exceed rated capacity — frame stress, hydraulic failure, wheel collapse
  • How to spec weight capacity for your facility, and when 150 kg is not enough

Every patient lift we build — manual, hydraulic, and electric — carries the same rating on the spec sheet: 150 kg weight capacity. Procurement managers see that number, compare it across suppliers, and usually move on. They should stop and ask what it actually means. A weight rating is only useful if you understand what it covers, what it does not, and how it maps to your residents.

What "150 kg weight capacity" actually means

When you see "150 kg weight capacity" on a patient lift spec sheet, you are looking at the rated load — also called the working load limit. This is the maximum weight the chair is engineered to lift, repeatedly, across its service life, while continuing to perform as the manufacturer specifies.

This is not the weight at which the chair breaks. The breaking point is significantly higher — typically 2 to 3 times the rated load — because every credible patient lift is engineered with a safety factor (more on that below). The 150 kg rating is the load at which the chair continues to lift smoothly, hold steady, and lower safely, cycle after cycle, for years of daily use.

This matters because procurement teams often confuse rated load with two other numbers: the maximum load the chair can lift once before failure (the ultimate load), and the load at which the chair will visibly bend, deform, or break (the breaking load). They are three different values, and conflating them leads to two opposite mistakes. The first is assuming "anything up to 150 kg is fine, 151 kg is not" — as if the rating were a cliff edge. The second is assuming "if it holds 150 kg it probably holds 200 kg, the rating is just conservative." The rated load is a working specification, and treating it as anything else gets people hurt.

The 3 numbers that matter: rated load, dynamic load, safety factor

When we engineer a patient lift at the factory, we work with three distinct numbers. Procurement managers should understand all three — they are not interchangeable, and a supplier who cannot tell you all three does not understand their product.

Definitions:

  • Rated (working) load — 150 kg for all three of our models (manual, hydraulic, electric). The load the chair is designed to lift continuously across its service life without performance degradation. This is the number on the spec sheet, the rating label, and the FDA certification documents.
  • Dynamic load — the transient peak force during a transfer. Almost always higher than the resident's static weight. A 100 kg resident who shifts suddenly during a lift, or whose caregiver pulls the chair into motion while the resident is seated, can generate dynamic loads of 120–140 kg momentarily. Dynamic load is the reason rated load is not the same as "maximum user weight."
  • Safety factor — the multiplier between rated load and ultimate load. For our chairs, the safety factor is engineered at 2.0× or higher. The frame is designed to survive a static load of at least 300 kg without structural failure. This is not a license to lift 300 kg — the hydraulic pump, casters, and locking mechanisms are not rated for that — but the frame itself will not fail below that point.

Rated (working) load Maximum load for repeated daily use across service life 150 kg Frame, hydraulic pump, casters, locking mechanisms Sustained loads above 150 kg, abuse, misuse

The 150 kg rating already includes an allowance for dynamic load. A 100 kg resident who shifts during a transfer is fine. What it does not include is sustained overload — and that is where the trouble starts.

How 150 kg maps to real resident populations

Spec sheets tell you what the chair can lift. They do not tell you whether that matters for your facility. To answer that, you need your resident weight distribution — and that distribution varies significantly by region, gender mix, and care setting.

A 150 kg patient lift covers approximately the 99.9th percentile of adult females and the 99.5th percentile of adult males globally. For a typical nursing home with 100 residents, the chair will safely lift every resident except possibly one. The question is whether that one resident is in your facility — and whether you have a documented plan for transferring them with the correct equipment.

What happens when you exceed rated capacity

This is the section most suppliers will not write. What happens at 160 kg, 180 kg, 200 kg? Nothing good, and nothing visible — at first.

Frame stress

The high-carbon steel frame is engineered to deform elastically under load, then return to shape. Up to the rated load, the deformation is small enough to be invisible and fully reversible. Above the rated load, the deformation starts to accumulate. After repeated overloads, the seat post develops a slight permanent set — a millimeter or two of tilt that you will not notice until it becomes a wiggle. By then, the frame has been work-hardened at stress points and is on its way to a fatigue crack at a weld.

Hydraulic failure

The hydraulic pump is rated for the same 150 kg. Above that load, two things happen. First, the relief valve in the pump can bypass — meaning the chair will not lift at all, the pedal goes down with no resistance. Second, the seal at the top of the cylinder experiences pressures above its design rating, which accelerates seal failure. A chair that is regularly overloaded will need a seal replacement in 12–18 months instead of the standard 24. On the electric model, the hydraulic motor is also working harder than spec, which shortens motor brush life.

Wheel collapse

The casters on our chairs are medical-grade, with bearings rated for 150 kg per chair (so roughly 600 kg total across four casters). Above the rated load per caster, the bearings begin to brinell — small dents form in the raceway that cause the wheel to develop a "spot," a position where it does not roll smoothly. Once brinelling starts, the wheel is on a slow path to seizure. The first sign is a click or a hop in the wheel's rotation. By the time you hear it, the bearing is already damaged.

⚠ Do not do this: Never use a 150 kg-rated patient lift for residents above 150 kg, even "just this once." The chair will probably not break immediately — which is exactly the problem. Overload damage is cumulative and invisible until a component fails mid-transfer, when a resident is on the chair and the caregiver had no warning. Use the correct equipment for the resident's weight, every time, without exception.

Bariatric transfers: when 150 kg is not enough

For residents above 150 kg, the answer is not "use a stronger patient lift" — it is "use the right equipment for the job." Bariatric transfer requires purpose-built equipment rated for the actual weight involved — typically 250 kg, 350 kg, or higher. Different frame geometry, different caster bearings, different hydraulic pump, different sling system. We do not manufacture bariatric lifts. We tell buyers this directly because we would rather lose a sale than sell equipment that gets misused. If your facility has a bariatric population, you need a bariatric lift from a different supplier; we will supply 150 kg-rated chairs for your standard residents.

What we can tell you is what does not work:

  • "Pushing the rating by 10 kg is fine." It is not. The chair will probably lift the resident, but the engineering safety margin is gone. You are operating with zero buffer for dynamic load — and dynamic load always shows up during a real transfer.
  • "Two caregivers can distribute the load." They cannot, not in a way that reduces the load on the chair. The chair lifts the resident's full weight regardless of how many caregivers are present. Extra caregivers reduce caregiver strain, not chair strain.
  • "Use a transfer board instead." A transfer board is a slide, not a lift. It does not reduce the load on the chair — it changes the transfer method entirely. For bariatric residents who cannot stand, a bariatric lift is the only safe option.

Procurement: how to spec weight capacity for your facility

Here is the process we recommend when deciding weight capacity for a fleet of patient lifts. Not glamorous, but it works.

  1. Pull your resident weight data. Most EHR systems can produce a weight distribution report per ward. Get the median, the 90th percentile, the 99th percentile, and the maximum resident weight. If you do not have this data, ask your clinical director — it is a 10-minute report.
  2. Choose a rated load that covers your 99th percentile. If your 99th percentile resident is 130 kg, a 150 kg-rated chair covers everyone except outliers and gives you a safety margin. If your 99th percentile is 150 kg or above, you need bariatric equipment for those residents in addition to standard chairs.
  3. Identify the bariatric residents by name. This sounds blunt because it is. Know which residents exceed the chair's rating and have a documented transfer protocol. Do not leave it to the caregiver on shift to make a judgment call under time pressure.
  4. Do not overspec to "be safe." A 250 kg-rated bariatric lift is heavier, wider, and harder to maneuver than a 150 kg lift. Using it for an 80 kg resident is unsafe for a different reason — it is harder to position correctly, which causes poor transfers and pressure points. Match the chair to the population, not the worst-case outlier.
  5. Re-evaluate every 12 months. Resident populations change. A chair that was correctly specced two years ago may be wrong for today's ward. Annual re-evaluation of weight capacity vs. resident profile is procurement hygiene.

Quick math:

A 100-bed nursing home with a 99th percentile resident weight of 130 kg can run a fleet of 150 kg-rated chairs and cover every resident safely. The same facility with one resident at 165 kg needs one bariatric lift for that resident — not a fleet of higher-rated chairs. Match the equipment to the actual population, not the worst case in the brochure.

Frequently asked questions

How do you test the 150 kg weight capacity at the factory?

Every chair goes through a static load test at 1.5× rated load (225 kg) held for 5 minutes, plus a dynamic cycle test of 1,000 lift cycles at full rated load (150 kg). The frame must show no permanent deformation after the static test, and the hydraulic system must show no pressure drop after the cycle test. These tests are documented in the FDA certification file for each model. We rate our chairs at 150 kg because that is the load we have proven they can lift repeatedly — not because it is a round number on a spec sheet.

We have a few residents over 150 kg. Can we use the chair for them occasionally?

No. The 150 kg rating is the maximum working load, not a soft suggestion. Using the chair for residents above that weight, even occasionally, removes the engineering safety margin and accelerates damage to the hydraulic system, casters, and frame. For residents over 150 kg, you need bariatric-rated equipment from a supplier that builds it. We do not, and we will tell you that directly rather than sell you the wrong product.

What happens if we put a 160 kg resident on the chair?

The chair will probably lift them — once. The hydraulic pump is over-engineered by a small margin, so a single 160 kg lift will not fail the chair immediately. But repeated 160 kg lifts will cause the hydraulic seal to fail in 12–18 months instead of 24, the casters to brinell, and the frame to develop fatigue at stress points. The damage is cumulative and invisible until a component fails mid-transfer. That is the worst-case scenario, and it is why the rating is a hard limit, not a guideline.

Does weight distribution on the seat affect the rated capacity?

Yes, but in practice the difference is small for normal transfers. A resident seated centered on the chair applies load close to the design distribution. A resident leaning heavily to one side applies asymmetric load that increases stress on one side of the frame and one pair of casters. The 150 kg rating assumes centered, stable loading. Significantly off-center loads should be avoided — and if a resident cannot sit centered, a different transfer method or a higher-capacity chair is the right answer.

Can we use the chair to transfer a resident with an IV pole or other attached equipment?

Yes, but the weight of attached equipment and IV fluid bags counts toward the rated load. A 140 kg resident with a 10 kg IV pole, fluid bag, and monitoring equipment is at the 150 kg limit — no margin for dynamic load. If the combined weight of resident and equipment exceeds 150 kg, do not use the chair. Transfer the equipment separately, or use a higher-capacity lift. The chair does not distinguish between resident weight and equipment weight — both load the frame, pump, and casters the same way.
