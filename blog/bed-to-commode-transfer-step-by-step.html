How to transfer someone from bed to a commode chair, step by step

The transfer is not the hard part — the measurement you should have taken last week is. This is the sequence caregivers actually run, the numbers that have to match, and the five mistakes that turn a routine move into an incident report.

What's in this guide

  • The four measurements to take before the first transfer
  • Room setup: what has to move, and what has to be locked
  • The eight-step transfer, in the order that keeps the resident's feet under them
  • The five mistakes that produce near-falls, and the fix for each
  • When to stop and use a sling hoist or a second caregiver
  • What to document if this happens twenty times a day

A patient lift transfer chair does not lift the person. It raises a split seat to mattress height, so a resident who can sit with support slides sideways across a level surface, and then the seat lowers over the commode. That means the entire transfer depends on two numbers being right: the mattress height and the chair's seat range.

If the mattress is inside the chair's range, the move is controlled and one caregiver can usually do it. If it is not, no amount of technique will fix it.

Measure these four things first

  1. Mattress height from the floor, at the working surface — not the frame. Usual home beds sit around 55–60 cm; hospital beds adjust.
  2. The chair's seat-height range. Manual chairs commonly span about 45–63 cm, hydraulic and electric about 46–66 cm. If your mattress top is above the maximum, this chair cannot do this bed.
  3. Frame width against the narrowest doorway on the route. The number that fails is the bathroom door: a 74 cm frame does not pass a 76 cm opening with a resident's hands on the armrests. Measure the opening, subtract two inches, then compare.
  4. Floor surface and threshold strips. Carpet adds drag; a 2 cm threshold under a caster is enough to stop a one-caregiver push. Walk the route before the first transfer, not during it.

Set the room before you touch the resident

  • Lower the bed to the height where the caregiver's feet are flat and their back is straight. Most transfers fail here: the bed is left high because it was raised earlier for care.
  • Clear the bed-side floor of the walker, the bin, the slippers — everything that a caster will hit at the moment you are holding someone.
  • Lock the bed wheels and the chair brakes. Both, every time. A chair that rolls 5 cm mid-slide is how a resident ends up on the floor.
  • Have the bucket in place before the transfer, not after. It is the difference between a completed toileting trip and a second trip.

The eight-step transfer

  1. Position the chair parallel to the bed on the resident's stronger side, seat edge touching the mattress, brakes locked. Seat level with the mattress or a centimetre below — never above.
  2. Raise the back of the bed so the resident is sitting, not lying, and let them settle for a moment. Rushing this step is what produces the grab.
  3. Help them turn toward you and bring both legs over the mattress edge, feet toward the floor.
  4. Confirm weight-bearing — can they take some load through their feet? If not, this is a two-person transfer or a hoist, and you stop here.
  5. Place their near hand on the chair's far armrest and your forearm under their far shoulder, so they lean toward the chair rather than away from it.
  6. Slide on a count — "one, two, three, slide" — with the resident pushing through their feet if they can. A count is not ceremony; it prevents the caregiver pulling before the resident is ready.
  7. Check they are fully on the seat before lowering: both buttocks on the split seat, nothing pinched at the opening, feet on the footrest.
  8. Lower the seat, release the brakes, and move — seat at the lowest comfortable height for the trip, armrests up on the way through doorways if the frame allows.

Five mistakes, and the fix for each

Mistake What it causes Fix
Brakes not set Chair rolls mid-slide; resident lands between bed and chair. Lock both devices before the resident moves, every time.
Bed left high Caregiver pulls from above, back takes the load. Lower the bed at the start, not when it gets difficult.
Seat above mattress level Resident has to climb up and across; the slide fails. Match heights, or slightly downhill toward the chair.
Pulling on the arm or the sleeve Shoulder injury, and the resident grabs the caregiver in reflex. Hands on the armrest and the shoulder blade, never on limbs.
No count Caregiver pulls before the resident pushes; both lose balance. Verbal count, every transfer, even at 3 a.m.

When to stop and change approach

Stop, and either get a second caregiver or switch to a sling hoist, if any of these is true: the resident cannot hold their trunk upright for the length of the slide; they cannot take any weight through their feet and the chair's seat is above the mattress; they are in pain during the sideways movement; their weight is close to the chair's tested rating; or the route includes a doorway you already know is marginal.

None of that is a failure of technique. It is the assessment telling you that the equipment and the person do not match — which is worth knowing before the transfer rather than after. For the selection logic behind that call, see Hoyer lift vs transfer chair.

If this happens twenty times a day

In a facility, the transfer is a process, not an event. Write the working heights on the care plan in centimetres, name the device by asset number so a shift change cannot substitute a different chair, record the number of staff required, and log near-misses with the time and the room. Three near-misses in the same bathroom is not a training problem; it is a 76 cm doorway and a 74 cm frame.

FAQ

What is the first thing to check before a bed-to-commode transfer?

Height. Measure the top of the mattress from the floor and the height of the commode or toilet seat, then confirm the chair's seat range covers the gap. A patient lift transfer chair works by raising its seat to mattress height so the resident slides across level — if the bed is higher than the chair's maximum, the transfer cannot be done with that chair.

Can one caregiver do a bed-to-commode transfer?

With a patient lift transfer chair, yes for a resident who can sit with support and assist slightly — that is what the seat-lift design is for. If the resident cannot hold their trunk, cannot follow instructions, or is close to the chair's rated capacity, use two caregivers or switch to a sling hoist. Local policy and the care plan decide, not the equipment alone.

How do you position the chair for the transfer?

Set the chair parallel to the bed with the seat edge touching the mattress and the brakes locked, on the side the resident can turn toward. The seat surface should be level with the mattress or slightly below it, never above — sliding downward is controllable, climbing upward is not.

What is the most common mistake in this transfer?

Leaving the brakes unlocked. The second most common is not lowering the bed to its lowest working height so the caregiver's feet are flat and their back is straight. The third is starting the slide before the resident's near arm is across the caregiver's shoulder, which turns a controlled slide into a catch.

How often should the commode bucket be emptied?

After every use for a shared chair, with the bucket washed and disinfected before it goes back on the chair. In a facility, the bucket is per-resident and labelled; the chair frame is shared. That is the hygiene model the design assumes.
