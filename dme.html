DME explained: what durable medical equipment is, who buys it, and how it gets paid for

DME is the acronym the whole sector runs on, and the one most guides never actually define. This page is the front door: the five tests that decide whether something is DME, the four categories buyers care about, how the money moves in the US, and where mobility equipment such as patient lift transfer chairs sits inside it.

DME stands for durable medical equipment

DME is the acronym for durable medical equipment — reusable medical equipment intended for use in the home. You will also see it written out as "durable medical equipment (DME)", abbreviated in job titles ("DME supplier", "DME billing"), and used as a general label for the whole trade ("the DME industry"). In the UK and Australia the same idea is usually called community equipment or assistive technology, but in the US market the acronym is the operating term.

The word that does the work in that definition is durable. It separates equipment from supplies. A wheelchair, a hospital bed, a patient lift transfer chair, a commode, or a walker is DME. Dressings, ostomy pouches, catheter trays, and test strips are supplies — they get used up, and they are bought, billed, and resupplied on a completely different rhythm. Confusing the two is one of the most common early mistakes in this sector, and it changes everything downstream: inventory turns, reimbursement rules, and how often the customer comes back.

The five tests that decide whether something is DME

In the US, the working definition comes from how payers classify equipment, and it comes down to five checks. A product that fails any one of them is not DME — it may still be a perfectly good product, but it will be bought and billed differently.

Test What it rules out
Durable — withstands repeated use Consumables and single-use items.
Medical purpose Comfort items and general household goods.
Not useful without illness or injury Products that a healthy person would buy anyway.
Used in the home Institutional-only equipment bought and billed by a facility.
Expected to last about three years or more Short-life devices and disposable accessories.

A patient lift transfer chair passes all five. It is steel frame, caster and mechanism — built for years of daily cycles, it exists to move a person who cannot safely transfer alone, it has no everyday use for an uninjured adult, it is explicitly designed for bedrooms and bathrooms in the home, and the working life is measured in years, not weeks.

The categories buyers actually shop by

Catalog taxonomy matters more than it looks. A DME supplier's website navigation, and its purchasing list, is usually organised into four or five buckets. If a manufacturer cannot say which bucket their product belongs to in one sentence, the buyer has to do that work — and usually does not.

  • Mobility. Wheelchairs, walkers, rollators, canes, transport chairs, and the transfer equipment that moves a person between them.
  • Patient handling and toileting. Patient lifts, patient lift transfer chairs, commodes, shower chairs, and bed rails. This is the category this site builds for.
  • Bathroom safety. Grab bars, tub benches, raised toilet seats, non-slip surfaces.
  • Beds and support surfaces. Hospital beds, pressure-relief mattresses, overlays.
  • Respiratory and other. Oxygen, CPAP, nebulisers, and the adjacent equipment that often shares a provider's billing system but not its warehouse.

Who buys DME

Three buyers, three completely different sales motions:

  • DME suppliers and distributors. They buy to resell or rent. They care about landed cost, reorder reliability, documentation, and whether the product can carry their own brand. Most of them already have a supplier for every category they carry — the question is whether you can be the second source.
  • Facilities. Nursing homes, assisted living, hospitals, and rehab centres buy for their own buildings, usually on annual contracts, and they care about service, training, and downtime above unit price.
  • Patients and families. They buy directly when insurance will not cover it or when they need it this week. They care about whether it fits through the bathroom door and whether one person can operate it — not about specifications.

A manufacturer that talks to all three as if they were one buyer wastes the conversation. The landed-cost argument is the distributor's language; the fit-and-dignity argument is the family's.

How the money works

In the US, most DME moves through insurance rather than cash. The common paths are Medicare Part B, state Medicaid programmes, and commercial plans, with the patient responsible for coinsurance and any unmet deductible. Three things control whether a supplier gets paid:

  • Medical necessity and a valid order. A physician has to document why this patient needs this equipment, and the paperwork has to match the item delivered.
  • Correct coding. Equipment is billed by code; the wrong code means a denial even when the equipment was appropriate.
  • Rental versus purchase rules. Some categories are rented on a capped-rental basis before ownership transfers, and resupply of accessories runs on its own schedule.

That machinery is what "benefit management" means in practice, and it is where most of a DME supplier's admin cost sits. We cover it in more detail in DME benefit management, explained.

Selling DME legally: what it takes, in outline

It is a regulated trade, and the requirements stack: a business entity, accreditation for the categories you bill, a surety bond in many states, state licensure where the state licenses DME at all, and enrolment with Medicare and each commercial payer you want to bill. States differ enough that copying another state's paperwork is a reliable way to lose a month. We walk through the sequence in how to start a DME business.

The practical consequence for a manufacturer is documentation. A supplier cannot resell your product into an insurance channel without a spec sheet that matches what ships, a load-test record, and a clear statement of regulatory status — registered, listed, or neither, stated precisely. Suppliers get audited on the paper trail, and they pass that requirement up the chain to you.

Where patient lift transfer chairs sit

A patient lift transfer chair is a seat-lift chair with a commode opening: the seat rises to bed height, the resident slides across, the seat lowers, and the chair is wheeled to the bathroom. It is not a sling hoist. That distinction matters commercially, because the two products are bought by the same buyer for different residents, and confusing them in a catalog costs you the order.

It also sits at an awkward spot in reimbursement: it is DME, but it is often bought with cash by families because the insurance pathway is slower than the need. That makes it a category where the product page, the spec sheet, and the photograph do more selling than any sales call. Vetting a manufacturer, if you are the buyer

Five questions separate a factory from a trading company, and they take one email to answer:

  1. Which parts do you make in-house? Welding, powder coating, and assembly are the three to ask about specifically.
  2. Can you send the load-test record? The test method and the number, not a logo.
  3. What is the exact regulatory status? Registered establishment, device listing, or nothing. Three different answers, three different meanings.
  4. What is the landed cost at my volume? FOB port, plus duty and freight, plus packaging — not a unit price.
  5. What breaks, and what do you ship to fix it? A supplier who knows their own failure points is a supplier who has run the product for years.

FAQ

What does DME stand for in healthcare?

DME stands for durable medical equipment: equipment that is reusable, serves a medical purpose, is not useful in the absence of illness or injury, and is appropriate for use in the home. In billing and procurement it is usually written as DME, and the supplier is called a DME provider or DME supplier.

What counts as durable medical equipment?

To count as DME, equipment generally has to meet five tests: it must withstand repeated use, have a medical purpose, not be useful to someone without an illness or injury, be used in the home, and be expected to last at least three years. Wheelchairs, hospital beds, walkers, commodes, patient lifts and patient lift transfer chairs all sit in this category. Consumables such as dressings or ostomy pouches are supplies, not DME.

Who buys durable medical equipment?

Three groups buy it. DME suppliers and distributors buy to resell or rent to patients. Care facilities (nursing homes, assisted living, hospitals) buy for their own buildings. Patients and families buy directly when the equipment is not covered or when they want it faster than the insurance process allows.

How does DME get paid for in the US?

Mostly through insurance: Medicare Part B, Medicaid, or commercial plans, with the patient responsible for coinsurance and any deductible. Payment depends on medical necessity, a physician order, and correct coding, which is why DME suppliers spend significant effort on documentation and benefit management. Cash sales and facility contracts cover the rest.

Is a patient lift transfer chair durable medical equipment?

Yes. A patient lift transfer chair meets the DME tests: reusable, medical purpose, used in the home, expected to last years. It is typically classified with patient lifts and commode chairs, and it is bought by DME suppliers for resale, by facilities for their own use, and by families paying directly.
